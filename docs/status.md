# Status: what is built, what is not, what it costs

The service is not for production yet. This page says what is built, what is left, what has been measured (each figure with its method) and the limits.

## What is built

* **Ingest and delivery.** `202` only after the flush that covers the event; requests that arrive together share one flush. Delivery to every subscribed endpoint with [Standard Webhooks](https://www.standardwebhooks.com) signatures (checked against the reference library), retries on the Standard Webhooks schedule, dead letters, and every outcome, with the time of the next attempt, surviving a crash ([delivery.md](delivery.md)).
* **Isolation.** Attempts do not hold the loop (up to 64 in flight, a state machine each), so a slow, silent or unreachable endpoint costs the others almost nothing: ingest stays at a median of 2.3 ms and healthy endpoints see their deliveries within milliseconds (`design.md` section 16). Each endpoint reads the events log from its own cursor and is bounded only by its own window; a **circuit breaker** pauses an endpoint whose every attempt has failed for 5 days (`breaker-days`, 0 turns it off), and its events wait in the log until `POST /endpoints/:id/enable` (`design.md` section 31).
* **Events and endpoints.** `Idempotency-Key`; replay to one endpoint or all; `410 Gone` disables an endpoint; with PostgreSQL the endpoints are a table, every ended attempt is a row, and an endpoint can be created, changed and deleted without a restart behind an admin token ([endpoints.md](endpoints.md)). Per endpoint: event types, a secret that rotates with two signatures, custom headers, a concurrency and a rate.
* **Cron.** A schedule appends an ordinary event at each scheduled second, exactly once, and fires once for the time the service was stopped ([cron.md](cron.md)).
* **Operating it.** `GET /readyz`, `GET /metrics`, the reason an attempt failed in the log, in `GET /events/:id/attempts` and in the history table, a graceful stop on `SIGTERM` or `SIGINT`, and a refusal to start on a corrupt log ([operating.md](operating.md), [runbook.md](runbook.md)). A log with damage in the middle, or a `delivery.seg` that is ahead of its `events.seg`, is refused at start with a status of its own (`--repair-logs 1` cuts the damage, keeping what it cut; `design.md` section 34.5).
* **Credentials.** Three bearer tokens (`ingest`, `read`, `admin`), and `production = 1`, which refuses to start in a configuration that is unsafe on the internet ([security.md](security.md)).
* **https and host names.** TLS 1.2 or 1.3 with the chain and the host name verified, every failure its own reason, sessions resumed; names resolved by the service with the address checked at every attempt ([https.md](https.md)).
* **Bounded logs.** Retention drops events, a segment at a time, only when they are final everywhere and old enough; a paused, disabled or dead endpoint, or a waiting replay, keeps its events (`retention-days`, 30 by default; `design.md` section 38, `retention.md`).
* **A database that comes back.** A lost connection is remade by the service and the start does not wait for the database (`design.md` section 37).
* **Dead letters.** Listed, replayed in bulk, a waiting replay cancelled; retries are spread by `retry-jitter` (`design.md` section 39).
* **Packaging.** Backup and restore of the two logs and the tables (`scripts/backup.sh`, `scripts/restore.sh`), a `Dockerfile` and a systemd unit (`deploy/`) ([runbook.md](runbook.md)).

## What it is not (yet)

Each item is from [`production.md`](production.md) and the design document, which also say how each was or was not verified.

* **Not for production.** The project's own list of what is left before that line is lifted: a **soak test of at least 24 hours under chaos** (kills, a dead endpoint, a slow database, with memory, descriptors and disk watched, and its numbers published), a **capacity page** that gives the method behind the figures under [Measured](#measured). The harness of the soak and its criteria, written before any run, are built ([soak.md](soak.md)), and so is the capacity page's method, with what short runs measured ([capacity.md](capacity.md)); no run of 24 hours has been made, the page's long-run cells are empty, and the figures under Measured come from short runs on shared machines. The soak's shakedowns found three things in the service (soak.md section 8): a restart delivered again events it had recorded as delivered (mended), a full data directory made it deliver the same events again and again (mended), and memory grew with every call that changes the database (mended).
* **OpenSSL, until lex-sys has its own TLS.** Delivery to an `https` endpoint drives OpenSSL in the process (32 foreign functions, all OpenSSL's and the only ones, pinned in `authority.json`; the authority report says `bounded: false` for them). It stays the default until lex-sys's own TLS has been independently reviewed (lex-sys#209). That TLS is built into a second binary, `hooks-pure` ([pure-tls.md](pure-tls.md)): the same tests pass on it, its authority report has **no foreign function and says `bounded: true`**, it has no session resumption, and a full handshake costs about 4 to 7 times the CPU on the machine measured. Not built for `https`: revocation checks, client certificates, IPv6, keeping a connection open between deliveries (each delivery costs a handshake), `/etc/hosts`, UDP or a second name server, a per-name allow-list for private addresses, an `https` endpoint at an IP address. A real public certificate chain was not exercised, only throwaway authorities.
* **The database.** A host *name* for `pg-host` is resolved by the service's own name server client, without holding the loop, and looked up again after a lost connection or a failed dial (design.md section 45). The `endpoints` table is read once: a change made behind the service's back, or one whose outcome it did not learn (a `504`), is seen at the next start. The history (`attempts`) is best effort; its rows older than `history-days` (30 by default) are deleted by the service.
* **Privacy and audit.** Erasure reaches neither the receivers' copies nor backups taken before; no index of bodies by person; the audit log is a file that whoever can write the data directory can edit; the encryption key is a file (no KMS). What the service does and does not do for GDPR and SOC 2 is [privacy.md](privacy.md).
* **Its own listener and its credentials.** No TLS for the service's own port (put a reverse proxy in front), no rate limit on failed tokens, a token changes only with a restart, and signing secrets and header values are stored in the clear: the database is the trust boundary.
* **Operating it.** One process, one thread, one core. An endpoint paused by the circuit breaker is resumed only by a person. An event dropped by retention is gone; its id answers `410` (a tombstone: ids are never given twice, so the service knows it was an event). The `Dockerfile` was built and run by hand and is not built in CI; the systemd unit was checked with `systemd-analyze` and never run under a real systemd; the release script does not sign, has no stable URL and writes a stub where an SBOM would be; the only binary is the CI artifact, for Linux x86-64; macOS is not verified.

## Limitations

* **One process, one thread, one core.** The loop does everything; up to 64 delivery attempts are in flight at once (8 per endpoint at most, fewer with a concurrency limit). **At most 1,024 endpoints** (an endpoint's id is still up to 999,999). It is a constant of the build (`max_endpoints` in `GET /stats`), not a setting. At the 1,025th, `POST /endpoints` is a `409` that says 1024 and stores nothing; a table or `endpoints.conf` with more than 1,024 endpoints ends the start with status 13, naming the row or the line. What it costs (`design.md` section 41.12): a start with 1,024 idle endpoints is 9.3 MB resident (9.1 MB with one), so an idle endpoint costs about 0.15 KB; an endpoint whose window is full of failed attempts costs its window, 40 KiB (1,024 of them: 50 to 52 MB resident); one whose table of dead letters is full costs up to 64 KiB more (1,024 of them: 73 to 97 MB, and 13 to 16 s to start). Both full at once is 125 MiB, computed: the two halves were measured apart.
* **Endpoints.** They come from a file read at start, or from the `endpoints` table (read at start, and added to with `POST /endpoints`). An endpoint the log has not seen (a row added by hand, a new line in the file) starts at the slowest cursor of the others, 0 if there are none; one created with `POST /endpoints` starts from now. They can be changed with `PATCH /endpoints/:id` (host, port, secret, event types, headers, concurrency, rate) and removed with `DELETE /endpoints/:id`: an attempt of it that is on the wire finishes first, and until it has the endpoint's slot cannot be given to a new one, so with 62 endpoints a `POST` in that window is a `409` for up to the attempt's deadline.
* **The table is read once.** A row changed behind the service's back is seen at the next start, and a change whose outcome the service does not know (a `504` "may have been stored") is not in the running service until then. A host *name* for `pg-host` is resolved by the service without waiting (the name server of `dns-server` or of /etc/resolv.conf), and again after a lost connection.
* **Destinations.** Only public IPv4 addresses are allowed by default, and a name must resolve to public ones (no ports limited, no per-host allow-list; `design.md` sections 26 and 40). Names are resolved over TCP, with no `/etc/hosts`, no IPv6, no UDP and one name server. The connection is closed after every delivery, so `https` costs a handshake each (resumed when the receiver allows); revocation is not checked.
* **Windows and the circuit breaker.** An endpoint is served at most 1,024 events past its own cursor (its window): an endpoint that is far behind waits there, the events beyond it wait in the log, and none of that holds up another endpoint (`design.md` section 31). Left alone, a dead endpoint dead-letters its events at the speed of its retry schedule, or is paused by the circuit breaker after `breaker-days` days of failures and waits for a person to enable it (no automatic resume).
* **Size and keys.** An event of 65,500 bytes or more is refused (`413`; 65,499 is the largest, less 11 and the length of its stored type, and less with an `Idempotency-Key`). At most `idem-keys` idempotency keys (262,144 by default) that are fresh; the index is rebuilt at start from the retained events only, and every fire of a schedule uses one; cron's keys are forgotten with their events.
* **Retention.** The events log is a chain of segments and a segment is deleted once every event in it is final everywhere and older than `retention-days` (and than `window-ms`); `delivery.seg` is replaced by a snapshot of the state it replays to when it has grown to `delivery-log-bytes`, which holds the loop for 40 to 70 ms with 62 endpoints whose windows are full (4.9 MB), about 0.5 s (473 to 581 ms) at 1,024 endpoints with every window full, and a few milliseconds for a typical state. Every file begins with a format version and an unknown version is refused. An event that is dropped is gone (`410`, which says retention dropped it; its idempotency key is forgotten, so the same key posted again is a new event). See `retention.md`.
* **Cron.** UTC only, at most 64 schedules, one service per database, and a jump of the machine's clock is a stop or a restart as far as a schedule can tell.
* **Dead letters and limits.** A dead letter list holds the newest 2,048 of an endpoint (the rest are completed from the log as room appears); a rate limit is memory and can be exceeded by a tenth of a second's worth across a restart.
* **Stopping.** The stop waits for no more than `stop-deadline-ms` and leaves the listening socket open until it exits; new connections are answered `503` and closed.

Not for production: [What it is not (yet)](#what-it-is-not-yet) says what is left before that line can be lifted.
